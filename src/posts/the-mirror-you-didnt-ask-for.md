*Spoiler note: this essay discusses a scene from Season 1, Episode 5 ("Pay Yourself First") of* Steal *on Prime Video.*

## The scene

In *Steal*, Zara is an ordinary back-office worker at a pension fund company. She jokes around, doesn't seem to take anything too seriously, and gets through the week the way many of us do. Then armed thieves storm her office and force her to help them move billions of pounds of people's retirement savings.

Later, she learns that two of her colleagues were secretly in on the plan from the start. So she goes to one of them, Milo, and asks the question that has been eating at her: they already had two people on the inside. Why drag *her* into it?

His answer is brutal. The thieves had asked for profiles of everyone in the back office — their personalities, their life stories, their psychology. Her colleagues handed over their impressions. **And from those impressions, Zara was picked.**

Not because she was skilled. **Because of how she was *seen*.** In Milo's telling, the people around her saw a woman in a dead-end job, going nowhere, partying every weekend to cope — the biggest mess in the office. Her cheerful, couldn't-care-less attitude? Nobody believed it. They saw an unhappy person pretending.

And that made her useful. If the plan needed someone to take the blame, she was the perfect fit. If she ended up dead and it looked like she couldn't live with her guilt, people would believe it. **Because of who she appeared to be.**

She was brought in as the mop to clean up the mess — chosen because, to the people around her, her life already looked like one.

Then Milo, of all people, tells her: "you've just had an outside perspective on how you're perceived. That's a blessing."

Coming from him, in that moment, it is cruel. Zara is crushed. She thought she was one person, and everyone around her saw someone completely different. She says it felt like she had been walking around exposed for years, and she was the only one who couldn't see it.

But underneath the cruelty, the line holds something true. That conversation is the turning point. It is where Zara stops letting other people decide what she is.

## The blessing in the brutality

I haven't stopped thinking about it.

**It is a blessing to have an external perspective of your being.** Truly. Most people wander through life entirely blind to how the world actually views them. You may see yourself one way, while the world sees you quite differently. To yourself, you may feel like you are running the show. To the world, you might look like a complete mess.

## Two portraits

Each of us carries two portraits.

The first is how we see ourselves internally — who we believe ourselves to be. It is shaped by our intentions, our private reasons, the effort nobody else saw, and the story we tell about why things went the way they did. In this portrait, we are usually doing our best, and doing it reasonably well.

The second is the portrait everyone else sees — how they perceive us from their own angle of view. **They can't see our intentions. They only see what we actually do — how we show up, what we finish, how we treat people,** what we say when we're under pressure. They perceive us from the outside, with less information but also with less bias.

Neither portrait is the full truth. **But when the two look very different, something important is hiding in that gap.**

## The world is not always right

Let me be careful here. The world's view of you is not always accurate. People misjudge. They project their own fears, envy and assumptions onto you. Some opinions are simply noise, and if you try to live by every one of them, you will lose yourself trying to please everybody.

So this is not an argument for handing your identity over to other people.

It is an argument for paying attention. Because while the world's perception may not always reflect who you truly are inside, **the mismatch between the two is invaluable.**

Sometimes we project the wrong image of ourselves without knowing it. Our inadequacies, our struggles, or simply the stage of life we're in can send a message we never meant to send — and the world mistakes our current condition for who we are.

That mismatch is a call to step up. Not to perform or pretend, but to grow — so that what people see begins to reflect who we truly are. **It births the continuous self-evaluation we need to keep growing.**

## Why the gap is a gift

**You cannot fix what you cannot see.** And some things about yourself you will never see from the inside.

You might believe you are a good listener while everyone around you feels interrupted. You might think you are patiently waiting for the right moment while others see someone who is stuck. You might feel organized in your head while your work looks chaotic to the people depending on it.

Or think about this: have you ever wondered why people in different places — at work, in school, in your community — don't seem to get along with you, even though you see yourself as easy-going? You may be right about your intentions. But if the same friction keeps showing up in different rooms, with different people, the world may be seeing something in you that you can't.

None of that is visible in the first portrait. **It only shows up in the second.**

That is why an honest outside view is a gift, even when it stings. It shows you the blind spots that your own mind is designed to protect you from.

## When the mirror is severe

Sometimes the outside view doesn't arrive gently. It arrives as failure, rejection, a hard conversation, or a moment of public embarrassment that you replay for weeks.

Something this severe stimulates growth. It forces you to shed the dead weight of who you used to be — the excuses, the habits, the version of yourself that was comfortable but going nowhere.

**It isn't meant to destroy you. It is meant to free you from a mediocre life.**

The pain comes from the old portrait cracking. What you do next decides whether you rebuild the same picture with better excuses, or paint a truer one.

## How to use the mirror

1. **Ask on purpose.** Don't wait for the world to force feedback on you. Ask a few people you trust: *What do I do that gets in my own way?* Then stop talking.
2. **Listen without defending.** Your first instinct will be to explain. Resist it. You are collecting information, not winning an argument.
3. **Look for patterns, not single opinions.** One person's view might be about them. The same comment from three different people is probably about you.
4. **Keep what's true, release what isn't.** Not every judgment deserves a place in your life. Take the part that helps you grow and leave the rest.
5. **Change one thing.** Insight without action is just another comfortable story. Pick one thing the mirror showed you and work on it.

## Back to the loop

In my last essay I wrote about the loop of learning: the more you know, the more you realize you don't know. The outside view is part of that same loop. Every honest reflection from the world shows you something you didn't know about yourself — and reminds you to stay humble about what you think you already know.

Zara's mirror came from the worst possible person, at the worst possible time, in the worst possible way. Most of ours won't be that dramatic. But they will come. You don't have to like what the mirror shows you. You only have to be brave enough to look, and wise enough to use it.
