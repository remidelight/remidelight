*The names in these stories are made up, but you will recognise every one of them.*

## When one favour becomes a lifetime account

Tunde earns a decent salary in Lagos. Two years ago, his younger cousin Kunle called in tears: his school fees were due and his father couldn't pay. Tunde sent the money. He was glad to help.

A few months later, Kunle called again — this time for a laptop. Then for house rent. Then for his mother's hospital bill. Then for "small money" to start a business. Each request came with a story, and each story was real enough.

What Kunle never asked was how Tunde himself was doing. He didn't know Tunde was helping three other relatives, paying his own rent, saving for a wedding, and quietly worried about his job. To Kunle, Tunde had become a source — not a person.

Eventually Tunde started ignoring calls. Kunle told the family Tunde had "changed" and "forgotten where he came from."

## Your helper also needs help

When someone helps you, it's tempting to believe they have plenty. After all, they helped once. Surely they can do it again.

But **the person who helps you is not a bank with infinite resources.** They have their own bills, their own family pressures, their own dreams on hold, their own bad months. Often, the people who help most are the ones carrying the most quietly.

Help is a gift, not a subscription. When we treat one act of kindness as a permanent account we can draw on, we don't just drain our helpers. **We teach generous people to stop being generous.**

A few simple ways to honour the people who help you:

- **Ask how they are**, not only what they can do for you.
- **Bring solutions, not just problems.** "Here's what I've tried, here's what's left" is very different from "Please, help me."
- **Say thank you — and show progress.** Nothing encourages a helper like seeing their help actually moved you forward.
- **Give back when you can,** even in small ways. Help that only flows one direction eventually dries up.

## "I go pay you next week"

Then there's borrowed money.

Aunty Bisi lent a friend ₦150,000 for an emergency. The friend promised to pay back in a month. A month became three. The friend stopped picking up. When Aunty Bisi finally saw her at a party, the friend acted offended: "Ah, is it because of that small money you are disgracing me?"

We all know this story. Somewhere along the way, we started treating repaying a loan as optional — and treating the lender's reminder as the rude part.

**When someone lends you money, they are trusting you with something they could have kept.** Paying back isn't a favour you do them. It's the minimum you owe.

- **Borrow only what you have a real plan to repay.**
- **Agree on a date — and keep it.** If you can't, tell them *before* the date, not after they chase you.
- **Pay in parts if you must,** but pay. Partial payment with honest communication keeps trust alive.
- **Never make the lender beg.** Chasing you should never be part of the arrangement.
- **Break the habit of borrowing "from here and there."** Constantly covering one loan with another is not a plan. It's a slow slide.

## "Aunty, it will be ready Friday"

The same principle applies to work.

Chioma gave her tailor fabric and a deposit for an outfit for her sister's wedding, three weeks ahead. "It will be ready Friday," he said. On Friday it wasn't started. On Tuesday he said "tomorrow." The night before the wedding she was in his shop at 9 p.m., watching him rush the seams.

Many self-employed people in Nigeria are skilled and hard-working. But too many have normalised a culture where customers must chase, beg and visit repeatedly before work is delivered.

**When someone pays you to do a job, delivering on time is part of the job.**

- **Give realistic dates,** not hopeful ones.
- **Communicate early** if there's a delay — before the customer has to call.
- **Finish what you've been paid for** before taking on more than you can handle.
- **Let your reliability be your marketing.** In a market full of excuses, the person who simply delivers on time stands out. As I wrote in *What Will People Say?*, reputation decides what people will trust you with next.

## The thread that ties these together

The cousin who keeps asking, the friend who doesn't repay, the tailor who must be chased — they look like three different problems. They're really one: **treating other people's trust, money and time as if they cost nothing.**

Every time someone helps you, lends to you, or pays you, they are placing a little trust in your hands. What you do with it decides whether they ever do it again — and whether they'll do it for the next person.

**Be the kind of person nobody has to chase.** Return what you borrowed. Deliver what you were paid for. And remember that the person who helps you might need a helper too.
