*The names in this story are made up, but you have met people like them.*

## The summer Bayo saw more

Bayo grew up in Ibadan. In his street, a "good life" looked a particular way: a steady job in a government office or a bank, a modest house eventually, a car by forty if things went well. Nobody told him that was the ceiling. It was simply all he had ever seen.

The summer after his second year in university, a cousin who worked in Lagos invited him to stay for a few weeks. One evening the cousin took him to a small meetup of young people building businesses — a woman who exported shea butter to three countries, a guy who had built an app used by thousands of traders, two friends who started a logistics company with one motorcycle and now had forty.

None of them were geniuses. None of them were born rich. What struck Bayo most was how *ordinary* they were. They spoke like him. Some had grown up in streets like his.

He went back to Ibadan different. Not because he had learned a new skill. Because the picture in his head of what was possible had changed. **What had seemed impossible now looked like a set of steps.**

## You can't aim at what you can't see

In my first essay, I wrote about the comfortable ceiling — why people settle for less than they are capable of. One of the biggest reasons is the simplest: **you cannot strive for what you have never seen.**

Ambition needs a target. If everyone around you lives a certain way, that way quietly becomes the edge of your imagination. You don't decide to aim low. You just never see anything higher to aim at.

This is why two equally talented people can end up in very different places. Often the difference isn't intelligence or effort. It's exposure — what each of them got to see, early enough, to believe it applied to them.

## Exposure isn't only about money

When people hear "exposure," they think of travel, expensive schools, or rich friends. Those help. But exposure is broader than that. It is seeing:

- **A different way of working** — someone who plans their week, tracks their money, or learns a new skill every year.
- **A different way of relating** — a couple who disagree without shouting, a boss who apologizes, a parent who listens.
- **A different way of thinking** — someone who asks "how could this work?" instead of "why it won't work."
- **A different scale** — a business that serves thousands, not tens; a career that crosses borders.

Every one of those can stretch your idea of normal. And once normal stretches, it rarely shrinks back.

## The danger of the small room

Exposure works both ways. If the only rooms you spend time in are small, your thinking stays small — not because you're limited, but because you're surrounded by limits that look like facts.

"That kind of thing is not for people like us."
"Nobody from here has done that."
"Just manage what you have."

Those sentences are rarely said with bad intent. They are often said with love, by people protecting you from disappointment. But **if you only ever hear what's impossible, you will spend your life inside it.**

## How to get more exposure — on purpose

You don't have to wait for a cousin in Lagos to invite you somewhere. You can go looking.

1. **Read about people slightly ahead of you,** not just celebrities. Their path is easier to imagine walking.
2. **Spend time in different rooms.** Attend a meetup, a seminar, a church or community event outside your usual circle. Sit quietly and listen.
3. **Ask people how they did it.** Most people who have built something are surprisingly willing to share — if you ask respectfully and specifically.
4. **Use the internet as a window, not just a distraction.** The same phone that shows you gossip can show you how someone built a business, learned a craft, or changed careers.
5. **Change one input.** One new book, one new friend, one new place. Small changes in what you see compound.

## Be someone's exposure

One last thing. Bayo didn't get his exposure by accident. A cousin chose to bring him along.

Somebody around you has never seen what you have seen — a younger sibling, a junior colleague, a neighbour's child. **The most powerful thing you can give them may not be money. It may be a picture of what's possible.**

Take someone with you into a bigger room. You may never know how far that one evening carries them.
