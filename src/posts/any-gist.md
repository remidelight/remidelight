*The names in this story are made up, but you have probably seen it happen.*

## Before she arrived

Funmi was hired into a team at a company in Victoria Island. Two days before she resumed, the gist had already arrived.

"Have you heard? The new girl got the job through connection."
"Somebody said she was very proud at her last place."
"She's coming in at a higher level than people who've been here for years. Watch her."

Nobody had met her. Nobody could say where the stories came from. But by the time Funmi walked in on Monday, smiling and eager, the team had already decided who she was.

They were polite, but cold. Nobody invited her to lunch. When she asked questions, people answered briefly and exchanged looks. When she made a small mistake in her second week, it was proof: "Didn't I tell you?"

Months later, a colleague finally got to know her properly. Funmi had been hired after three rounds of interviews. She had no connections at the company. She wasn't proud — she was shy, and trying hard to fit in.

By then, she had already started looking for another job.

## Gist feels harmless

In Nigeria, gist is part of daily life. It's how we bond, pass time, and feel included. "Come and hear gist" is an invitation to friendship. And much of it is harmless — news, stories, laughter.

But there's a kind of gist that isn't harmless at all: talk about people who aren't there, built on things nobody has checked. That's what this essay is about.

## It steals your time

Think about how many hours go into gist in a week. On WhatsApp groups. At the office kitchen. On phone calls that start with "Did you hear about…" Twenty minutes here, an hour there.

It doesn't feel like much, because it doesn't feel like work. But **time spent discussing other people's lives is time not spent building your own.**

An hour a day of gist is more than 360 hours a year. That's enough time to learn a new skill, start a small business, read dozens of books, or build a relationship that actually matters. The real cost of gist isn't only what it says about others. **It's the progress you never made because you were busy talking.**

## It builds enemies out of strangers

The quieter damage is what gist does to how we see people.

When you hear something negative about someone before you meet them, you don't meet *them*. You meet the story. You read their silence as pride, their confidence as arrogance, their mistake as character. **Gist creates prejudice — a judgment formed before the evidence.**

And it spreads. The person who heard the gist repeats it to someone else, who has never even seen the subject. Soon people are condemning someone they have never spoken to, based on something no one can trace back to a fact.

That's how hatred is built: not in one big moment, but in many small conversations about someone who isn't in the room to defend themselves.

## It degrades the society

Zoom out, and the cost grows.

When gist replaces facts, a society loses its ability to tell what's true. Rumours travel faster than corrections. A broadcast message forwarded without checking can damage a reputation, a business, or a whole community in one afternoon. People stop trusting each other — and as I wrote in *Before We Blame the Government*, **a society without trust is a society that can't move forward.**

Gist that isn't based on facts doesn't just hurt individuals. It slowly poisons the air everyone breathes.

## A better way

You don't have to become the person who never laughs or never talks. You just have to become more careful with what you carry and pass on.

1. **Pause before you pass it on.** Ask: *Is it true? Do I know it first-hand? Does repeating it help anyone?* If the answer is no, let it stop with you.
2. **Meet people for yourself.** When you hear something about someone, treat it as a rumour, not a verdict. Form your own view from your own interaction.
3. **Redirect the conversation.** "Have you actually spoken to her?" or "Let's not judge before we know her" can quietly change the room.
4. **Speak *to* people, not *about* them.** If something bothers you about a colleague or friend, talk to them, not to everyone else.
5. **Count your gist hours.** For one week, notice how much time goes into talking about others. Then decide what you'd rather build with that time.

## Back to Funmi

Funmi's story didn't need a villain. Nobody set out to hurt her. It was just gist — a few casual conversations, repeated without thinking.

That's what makes it dangerous. **The damage of gist rarely comes from cruel people. It comes from ordinary people who never stopped to ask whether what they were saying was true.**

Be the place where the rumour stops. Spend the time you save on building something of your own.
