*The people in this essay are composites. The names are made up. But if you have lived in Lagos, you have met every one of them.*

## A day in Lagos

Tolu takes his car to a mechanic in Ladipo for new brake pads. A simple job. He's told to come back in the evening.

When he picks it up, the brakes are fine. But a week later, the car starts making a sound it never made before. Another mechanic opens the bonnet, shakes his head, and asks, "Who worked on this car? Your alternator is not the original one." Somewhere between the morning and the evening of that "simple job," a good part was removed — likely to be sold into somebody else's car — and a tired one was put in its place.

The same month, Tolu hires a plumber. The list of materials comes back with prices higher than anything he finds when he checks the market himself. "Oga, na so price be now," the plumber says.

His sister is building a small house in Ikorodu. She can't leave the site for one day, because she has learned that when she isn't there, the cement ratio quietly changes. One bag becomes "enough" where three were needed. The difference goes somewhere.

At Computer Village, a friend drops off a phone to fix a cracked screen. It comes back with the screen fixed — and the battery and some original parts swapped for cheaper ones. He only finds out months later.

At an office where he goes to process a document, a clerk tells him his file "is moving slowly." For a small something, it can move faster.

None of this is dramatic. That's the point. **It's ordinary.**

## The easy target

When we talk about what's wrong with Nigeria, we usually point upward. The government. The politicians. The people who loot billions while roads crumble and hospitals lack basic equipment.

And we should hold leaders accountable. Leadership matters enormously. When people at the top steal without consequence, it sends a message all the way down.

But here is the uncomfortable part: the mechanic in Ladipo is not in government. Neither is the plumber, the contractor, the phone repairer, or the clerk who "helps your file move." **The dishonesty we complain about in Abuja is the same dishonesty we practice in the workshop, on the building site, and in the office.** Leaders don't come from another planet. They come from us.

Before we blame the government, we have to admit that trust has eroded at every level of our society — including ours.

## What lost trust costs us

When you can't trust anyone, everything becomes expensive.

You can't just hire a mechanic; you have to stand over him. You can't just give a contractor money; you have to buy the materials yourself and watch them being used. You can't send someone to the market for you; you have to go yourself. You can't build a business that grows, because you can't delegate anything to anyone without checking it twice.

**A society without trust is a society that can't scale.** Every transaction needs supervision. Every agreement needs a witness. Every naira spent comes with the fear that some of it is being stolen. We pay for dishonesty not only in what is taken, but in all the time, energy and opportunity we lose guarding ourselves against it.

Trust is invisible infrastructure. When it breaks down, everything built on it gets weaker.

## When the honest one looks mad

The saddest part isn't that people cheat. People have always cheated. The saddest part is how normal we have made it.

Think of Kunle, a young man in an office where everyone takes a cut. A supplier inflates an invoice and offers him his "share" to sign it. He refuses. Word gets around. His colleagues don't see integrity. They see a fool.

"This one no get sense."
"Na your papa money?"
"Is he okay at all?"

**In a society where dishonesty is normal, honesty starts to look like madness.** The person who refuses to steal is treated as naive, stubborn, or even mentally unwell. He becomes the odd one out — and slowly, the pressure to fit in becomes the pressure to cheat.

That is how a culture rots: not when people do wrong, but when doing right becomes embarrassing.

## Understanding, not excusing

To be fair, many people aren't cheating because they are evil. Life in Lagos is hard. Prices rise faster than incomes. Many feel they have been cheated by the system, by employers, by customers who refuse to pay. So they cheat back. "If I don't take it, someone else will." It feels like survival.

I understand that. But understanding is not the same as excusing. Hardship explains why the pressure is strong. It doesn't make the choice right. And every time we choose to dupe someone, we make the society a little harder for everyone — including ourselves and the people we love.

## Where change actually starts

We can't fix the whole country tomorrow. But trust is rebuilt the same way it was lost — one transaction at a time.

1. **Be the exception.** Do honest work, even when no one is watching and even when everyone else is cutting corners.
2. **Honesty goes both ways.** Pay artisans fairly and on time. A customer who underpays or "owes" a worker for months is part of the same broken system.
3. **Reward the honest ones.** When you find a mechanic, contractor or repairer who is truthful, give them your loyalty and your referrals. Make honesty profitable. (This is where reputation — the subject of my last essay — becomes a force for good.)
4. **Stop laughing at integrity.** When someone refuses to take a cut, don't call them foolish. Respect them out loud. Culture changes when we change what we admire.
5. **Teach it at home.** Children learn what's normal from what they see. Let them see you return the extra change.

## A last word

It is easy to blame the people at the top, and they deserve much of the blame. But the country is not only shaped in Abuja. It is shaped in Ladipo, in Computer Village, on building sites in Ikorodu, and in every small office where a file "moves faster" for a fee.

**If we want a society where we can trust our leaders, we have to become people who can be trusted ourselves.**
