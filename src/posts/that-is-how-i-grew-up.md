You have probably heard it. You may have said it.

*"That's just how I grew up."*
*"That's how I was trained."*
*"In my house, we didn't do all that."*
*"You don't know what I went through."*

Someone shouts at every disagreement, because that's how arguments sounded at home. Someone never says sorry, because nobody ever said sorry to them. Someone can't keep money, because growing up there was never any to keep. Someone trusts no one, because the people who should have protected them didn't.

Every one of those explanations may be true. And every one of them can quietly become a prison.

## A brother and a sister, one house

*The names in this story are made up, but the story is one many of us know.*

Segun and his younger sister Teni grew up in the same small flat in Mushin. Their father was a hard man. When he was angry, the whole compound knew. He shouted, he broke things, and he never apologized. Their mother absorbed it all. She kept the peace by keeping quiet, swallowed her words, and cried where she thought the children couldn't hear.

Twenty years later, both children are grown. And both are still living in that flat, in a way.

Segun has lost three jobs. Not because he isn't smart — he is — but because every disagreement with a manager ends with him raising his voice. His wife has stopped arguing with him; she just goes silent, the way his mother used to. When his friends gently tell him he needs to calm down, he waves it off: *"That's how I grew up. That's how I am. Take me or leave me."*

Teni went the other way. She watched what her mother endured and promised herself no man would ever do that to her. That promise kept her safe as a girl. As a woman, it has hardened into something else. She expects the worst from every man she meets. When a kind, patient man named Kola came into her life, she tested him, pushed him away, and read every small mistake as proof of what she already believed. He eventually left. *"I knew it,"* she said. *"All men are the same. I saw it growing up."*

Two children from the same house. One copied the wound. The other built a wall around it. Both say the same thing: *that's how I grew up.* And both are letting a man who no longer controls their lives keep controlling them anyway.

The turning point for Teni came quietly. A friend asked her one evening, *"Teni, are you punishing Kola for what your father did?"* She didn't have an answer. For the first time, she saw that her bitterness, which had once protected her, was now costing her the very life she wanted. She started talking to a counselor. She is learning — slowly, awkwardly — to judge the man in front of her by what he does, not by what her father did.

Her past explains her bitterness. It doesn't have to decide her future.

## Your background is real

Let me start where honesty demands.

Where you came from shapes you. The home you grew up in, the poverty or comfort around you, the things you saw, the things done to you, the opportunities you never had — these leave marks. Some people carry wounds that took years to form and will take years to heal. Pretending otherwise is cruel.

So this is not an essay telling anyone to "just get over it."

**But understanding why you are the way you are is the beginning of the work, not the end of it.**

## An explanation is not a permission

There is a difference between an *explanation* and a *permission*.

"I grew up in a house where people shouted" explains why you shout. It does not give you permission to keep shouting at your partner, your children or your colleagues for the rest of your life.

"I was never taught how to manage money" explains why you struggle with it. It doesn't excuse you from learning now, when the information is available and the choice is yours.

The danger of "that's how I was raised" is that it sounds like a reason but works like a verdict. It closes the conversation. It turns your past into your permanent identity. And it hands control of your future to people and circumstances that are no longer in the room.

## The math of the rest of your life

Here is something that changed how I think about this.

If you are in your twenties, and you live into your seventies, you have lived roughly **one-third** of your life. Most of that first third was decided for you. You didn't choose your parents, your neighborhood, your schools, or the way people spoke to you when you were small.

**But you still have two-thirds left. And those two-thirds will be decided mostly by you.**

Your first third was written largely by other people. The next two are yours to write. It would be a strange thing to let the shortest, least chosen part of your life control the longest, most chosen part.

Even if you are older, the principle holds. Whatever time is left is still the part you get to direct.

## How the past keeps steering

Most of the time, the past doesn't control us dramatically. It controls us through habits we never questioned:

- The way we react when we feel disrespected.
- What we believe about money, success and people "like us."
- What we think we deserve.
- How we handle conflict, failure and criticism.

These patterns feel like personality. Often they are just inheritance — things we picked up before we were old enough to choose. And inheritance can be examined, kept or returned.

## Taking the wheel

1. **Notice the phrase.** Every time you hear yourself say "that's how I am" or "that's how I was raised," pause. Ask: *is this a reason, or an excuse?*
2. **Separate the explanation from the decision.** Keep the understanding — it builds compassion for yourself. But make the next decision based on who you want to become, not who you were trained to be.
3. **Pick one inherited habit to unlearn.** Not ten. One. The temper, the silence, the spending, the distrust. Work on it deliberately.
4. **Find new models.** You can't become what you've never seen. Spend time around people who handle life differently from how it was handled around you. (This is the exposure I wrote about in my first essay.)
5. **Get help when the wound is deep.** Some patterns come from real trauma. There is no shame in talking to a counselor, a mentor, or someone you trust. Healing is work, not weakness.
6. **Be patient, but don't stop.** You won't undo twenty years of habit in a month. That's fine. You have two-thirds of a lifetime.

## You are someone's background

One last thing.

Someday, someone will say about you: *"That's how I was raised."* Your children, your nieces and nephews, the young people who watch how you live. **Whatever you don't confront in yourself, you will hand down to them.**

That's the real power of the two-thirds. It's not only your future you're writing. It's the first third of someone else's.

Segun's children are growing up the way he did. **Teni is deciding that the bitterness stops with her.**

Your background explains where you started. It doesn't get to decide where you finish.
